An Essay in Language & Misidentification

The Turkey's
Passport Problem

How one bird from Mesoamerica ended up carrying a different country's name in every language on earth.

There is a bird, native to the forests of Mexico and Central America, that has spent five centuries being named after the wrong place in virtually every language on earth. The English call it a , after the Ottoman Empire. The Turks call it , after India. Hindi speakers call it , after Peru. The Dutch call it , after a port city in India. Malay speakers call it , "Dutch chicken." Khmer says , "French chicken." And Scottish Gaelic, independently and with some geographical imagination, arrived at , also meaning French chicken. Spanish, remarkably, went with , the Latin word for peacock.

Not one of these languages points to Mexico or Central America, where the animal actually lives. Not one names the continent it came from. Every language reached for something it already knew, a familiar place just over the horizon of the unknown, and planted the bird's name there.

Mexico and Central America remain empty, unlit. A single label pulses there: "Origin." Last pin to drop: a small turkey icon over Mesoamerica. It glows gold.

What happened to this bird is one of the stranger stories in the history of language: a comedy of errors played out across five centuries, six continents, and a cast of explorers, merchants, and navigators who were collectively very confident and almost entirely wrong. The turkey is, in this sense, a linguistic crime scene. Every name it carries is someone else's mistake, fossilized into vocabulary. And the chain of mistakes, if you follow it carefully, forms a perfect circle.

❧ I ❧

Where the Bird Actually Comes From

Before the errors: the facts. There are two living turkey species. The wild turkey, Meleagris gallopavo, ranges across a wide stretch of North America, the eastern and southwestern United States, and south into Mexico. The ocellated turkey, Meleagris ocellata, is a smaller, iridescent cousin found only in the Yucatán Peninsula of Mexico and the adjacent parts of Belize and Guatemala.

The bird Europeans encountered and carried home was the domesticated form of the wild turkey, and its domestic history begins specifically in central Mexico. The earliest clear evidence of domestication, around 800 BCE, comes from Mesoamerican archaeological sites in what are now the Mexican states of Jalisco, Guerrero, and Veracruz. These were Aztec birds. The Aztecs called them huehxōlōtl in Nahuatl, a word that survives in the Mexican Spanish regional term guajolote, still used today in many parts of Mexico to mean turkey.

Visual note, Origin map inset A zoomed map of Mexico highlights the domestication zone in the central-southern states. A secondary inset shows the broader wild turkey range across North America. Caption: "Wild range: most of North America. Domestic origin: central Mexico. Confusion: global."

A second, independent domestication happened later, around 200 BCE, among the ancestral Pueblo peoples of the southwestern United States, in what is now the Four Corners region of Colorado, Utah, Arizona, and New Mexico. These birds were kept primarily for their feathers, used in ceremonial blankets and regalia, not primarily as food.

The birds that Spanish conquistadors took back to Europe after 1519 were the Mexican ones, the Aztec-domesticated birds from central Mexico. All of the world's commercial turkeys today descend from that single Mexican lineage. The bird is native to North America as a species, but the one that went global was specifically Mesoamerican, specifically Aztec. That distinction matters, because no language that named it got even that close.

❧ II ❧

How the English Got It Wrong

The story of the naming begins not with the turkey at all, but with a different bird entirely.

Visual note, Two birds side by side Illustrated portraits of a helmeted guinea fowl (left) and a wild turkey (right). Both have bare-skinned heads, both are large, speckled, and strutting. A bracket underneath reads: "These are not the same bird. Europe thought otherwise."

The helmeted guinea fowl is a speckled, bare-headed bird native to sub-Saharan West Africa (and here, in a small dry run of the same logic to come, it was named "turkey-cock" and "turkey-hen" after the Ottoman trade route it arrived through, not after sub-Saharan Africa where it actually lived). In the early sixteenth century, guinea fowl were being imported into Europe through the Ottoman trade network, via the lands broadly known as "Turkey." Europeans gave them the logical name: turkey-cock, turkey-hen. These were birds that came from the Turks.

Then, sometime around the 1550s, English sailors returning from the Americas brought home a larger, unfamiliar bird with a bare red wattle, a fan of bronze and copper tail feathers, and a sound best described as an insistent gobble. It looked, to someone who had not been paying close attention, somewhat like the guinea fowl. English settlers transferred the name. "Turkey" migrated from an African bird to an American one, by way of a trade route through Constantinople.

The error was noticed fairly quickly. Once it became clear the two birds were unrelated species, the names were supposed to separate. They did not. The American bird kept . Words, once coined, resist correction. Ireland and Wales borrowed the same mistake wholesale: Irish , Welsh . Both absorbed the English error and moved on.

When English settlers in the New World found wild turkeys roaming the forests of Virginia and Massachusetts, they were encountering the same species the Aztecs had domesticated a thousand years earlier. They called the wild bird by the same wrong name as the domestic one. The error had traveled all the way around the world and landed back in the country where the bird was from. Nobody noticed.

❧ III ❧

How the Spanish Looked at a Turkey and Saw a Peacock

The Spanish conquistadors were the first Europeans to actually see the bird in its homeland. Hernán Cortés entered the Aztec capital Tenochtitlan in 1519 and encountered the domesticated turkey in Aztec farms and markets. Here, at last, was someone with direct information about where the bird came from.

They named it . The Latin word for peacock.

The peacock, a bird from South and Southeast Asia, had been known in Europe since antiquity, prized for its spectacular fan of iridescent tail feathers. The conquistadors looked at the turkey, large, impressive, fan-tailed, a grand bird by any measure, and apparently reached for the most extravagant bird they already knew. The turkey was the new peacock. It got the peacock's name.

The consequence of this is surreal. In modern Spanish, the actual peacock is now called pavo real, while the turkey kept plain pavo. A bird documented in European language since ancient Greece was demoted by a bird from Mexico that Spaniards had only just met.

But the word real earns a pause here, because Spanish does something that no other language quite manages: it makes the same word carry two entirely different meanings simultaneously. Real meaning "royal" comes from Latin regalis, the root of "regal" and "regent," the word that gives football clubs like Real Madrid their honorific title (granted by King Alfonso XIII in 1920, meaning Royal Madrid, not, as English speakers sometimes assume, "actual Madrid"). Real meaning "genuine" or "actual" comes from an entirely separate Latin root, res, meaning "thing," the root of "reality." Two different words, identical in spelling, sharing a language.

So pavo real is simultaneously "royal peacock" and "the real peacock," the genuine one, the original, the one that was here before the imposter arrived from Mexico. Both readings are correct. Both meanings apply. The peacock had to declare itself both royally and actually a peacock, as if the mere fact of being a peacock were no longer self-evident. The turkey did not have to declare anything. It simply kept the name and carried on.

There is one further dimension to this that Linnaeus (Carl Linnaeus, the Swedish botanist and zoologist who created the modern system of scientific naming for all living things) locked into place in 1758, when he gave the turkey its official scientific name. The genus he assigned, Meleagris, is Greek for guinea fowl, named after the mythological figure Meleager, whose grieving sisters were transformed by the goddess Artemis into guinea fowl. The species name, gallopavo, is a compound Latin word: gallus (rooster or fowl) and pavo (peacock). So the full scientific name of the bird translates, in the language of science, as something in the range of "guinea-fowl that is a rooster-peacock." It is named after three birds it is not. The confusion did not stop with folk language. It was formalized, in Latin, by one of the most authoritative naturalists in history, and it has been authoritative ever since.

And here the story completes a circle that most accounts of this subject miss entirely. Go back to the beginning: the reason English speakers call the bird "turkey" in the first place is that sixteenth-century English sailors confused the American bird with the guinea fowl, the African bird that arrived in Europe via Ottoman trade routes. The guinea fowl was already called the "turkey-cock," and the American bird looked similar enough, so it inherited the name. The guinea fowl is the original source of the error.

Now consider what Linnaeus named the American bird in 1758: Meleagris. The Greek word for guinea fowl.

The same confusion, made twice, two hundred years apart, by different people, in different languages, for different reasons, arriving at the same wrong bird. English folk naming and formal Latin taxonomy both reached independently for the guinea fowl and attached it to the turkey. Linnaeus was not aware he was repeating a mistake already embedded in English. He was simply using the nearest Greek term for a large gallinaceous bird. But the effect is identical: the turkey's most authoritative name, the one used in every biological classification, every scientific paper, every museum label in the world, is built on the same misidentification that gave it its most common name. The confusion is not incidental to the turkey's identity in human language. It is constitutive of it. From the first English sailor to the greatest taxonomist of the eighteenth century, everyone who tried to name this bird named a different bird instead.

Latin American Spanish has a richer set of regional terms that get closer to the original: (Mexico), and chumpe (Central America), . These words have indigenous roots and no geographical confusion. They are also, notably, the words that survived where the bird was actually domesticated.

But pavo itself traveled. Spain colonised the Philippines in 1565 and brought turkeys with it aboard the same ships, introducing the bird to the archipelago barely a generation after the Spanish had first encountered it in Mexico. The Tagalog word is a direct phonetic borrowing of Spanish pavo, arriving with the bird itself in the mid-sixteenth century. The peacock error, made by conquistadors standing in Mexico, eventually sailed across the Pacific and became the standard word for turkey in the language of over twenty million people in Southeast Asia. Today, younger Filipinos are more likely to associate the word pabo with Korean slang for "stupid" than with any bird, and most simply use the English word "turkey" instead. The Spanish name is losing ground to the English name, which is itself named after the wrong country.

❧ IV ❧

How the Turks Got It Wrong Differently

In Turkish, the word for turkey is . This means "from India," a reference to the widespread European belief in the sixteenth century that the Americas and India were the same place, or at least in the same direction. Columbus had sailed west looking for India and found the Caribbean instead. Operating on the assumption he had arrived approximately where he intended, he called the people he met "Indians." That particular error was so thoroughly distributed that it shaped the vocabulary of a continent.

Turkey became hindi in Turkish. In French, the bird became , contracted from poulet d'Inde, "chicken of India." A whole family of languages followed:

India had nothing to do with any of this. The turkey is from Mexico. But the Columbus error was so perfectly synchronized across so many languages simultaneously that it became its own kind of fact: a vast linguistic family all pointing, in unison, at the wrong subcontinent.

❧ V ❧

How the Portuguese Introduced a Third Wrong Country

It was the Portuguese who added Peru to the confusion, and in doing so set off a chain reaction that would eventually reach all the way to a small Polynesian island most people cannot find on a map.

The Portuguese encountered the turkey around the same time that Francisco Pizarro was invading the western coast of South America, the territory then being named Peru. In the general excitement and geographical fog of sixteenth-century exploration, the Portuguese concluded that the bird must have come from there. They named it .

This was wrong. The bird does not come from Peru. But the name traveled:

The Hawaiian case has its own story. The nobleman Boki, on the South American leg of a world voyage in 1824, acquired live turkeys in the port of Valparaíso, Chile. He brought them back to Hawaii along with the Portuguese name peru, which became pelehu in Hawaiian. A few years later the birds and the name traveled on to the small Polynesian island of Rotuma, where they became perehu. A naming error made in Lisbon in the 1520s, based on a confusion about South American geography, eventually echoed in a language spoken by fewer than two thousand people on a small volcanic island in Fiji, 650 kilometres north of Suva, called Rotuma.

❧ VI ❧

How the Dutch Named It After a City

The Dutch were more specific in their wrongness. Their word, , comes from Calcoen-hoen, "hen of Calicut." Calicut, now called Kozhikode, is a port city on the Malabar Coast of Kerala in southern India, a crucial hub in the Portuguese spice trade. For sixteenth-century Dutch merchants, Calicut was a synonym for "the exotic trading East." They pointed at it specifically rather than at India in the abstract, and in doing so created the most geographically precise wrong name in the entire family.

❧ VII ❧

The Other Theories: France, Rome, and Ethiopia

Not every language blamed the same wrong continent. A final cluster pointed at Europe itself.

Khmer and Scottish Gaelic both mean "French chicken," apparently because French merchants were the first to bring the bird to those regions. Whoever sold you the turkey got the credit. Greek carries a similar connotation. Standard Arabic means "Roman/Byzantine rooster." Palestinian Arabic took a separate path: , "Ethiopian rooster," since Abyssinia was known as Habash in Arabic. Egyptian Arabic uses dīk yūnānī in some dialects, meaning "Greek rooster," named after Greece.

The Nahuatl name the Aztecs used, , is the only name in common use that points, without confusion or error, toward the place where the bird was actually from. It is the name that did not travel.

"The only name that got it right is the only one that stayed home."

❧ VIII ❧

The Languages That Gave Up on Geography Entirely

Not every language reached for a map when it needed to name the bird. A significant and underappreciated cluster of languages looked at the turkey and described, with precision, exactly what they saw or heard. These names contain no false claims about where the bird is from. They simply report the experience of encountering it, and they are, in their own way, the most honest names in this entire story.

Two languages named it for how it arrived. Albanian has two forms: , sea rooster, and , sea hen. Slovak uses (male) and (female), from more, the Slovak word for sea. These languages did not ask where the bird was from. They noted how it got there: across the sea. That much was demonstrably true, and it is more than can be said for any other name in this article.

A second cluster named it for the sound it makes. Italian renders the turkey's sharp staccato call as tak-tak-tak with a diminutive suffix. German combines trut, the call farmers used to lure turkeys, with Hahn, rooster. Persian is widely considered the most phonetically faithful rendering of an actual turkey gobble in any language. James Harbeck at The Week called it a more accurate rendition than the Italian, though he noted he had never personally owned a live turkey and could not be entirely certain.

A third cluster named it for what it looked like, and here the descriptions grow unexpectedly precise. Mandarin Chinese means fire chicken, for the bird's vivid red head. Japanese and Korean both mean seven-faced bird, reflecting the male turkey's ability to change the colour and shape of its face dramatically with mood. Thai means trunk chicken, comparing the hanging wattle to an elephant's trunk. Burmese and Urdu both mean elephant bird or elephant chicken, the same wattle-based observation arrived at independently on opposite sides of the continent.

Mandarin also produced, in parallel, what may be the single most vivid name for the turkey in any language: tushouji (吐綬雞), "cough-up-a-ribbon chicken," referring to the way the wattle hangs and waggles from the bird's beak. No European language, in five centuries of naming, came close to that level of observational specificity.

❧ IX ❧

The Circle Closes

Visual note, Full chain map All map pins now lit simultaneously. Label appears: "The bird never moved. The names went around the world."

What the Dutch name after Calicut, Hindi speakers name after Peru. What the English name after Turkey, the Turks name after India. And what do Hindi speakers, of the language that the Turks named the bird after, call the bird?

They call it piru. After Peru.

English names it after Turkey. Turkish names it after India. India names it after Peru. Croatian and Slovenian name it after Peru via Italy. Hawaiian names it after Peru via Portugal, carried by a nobleman from Valparaíso. Malay names it after Holland. And Holland named it after a city in India that is not where the bird came from either.

The final, most complete absurdity: in modern Hindi, the word for turkey is sometimes rendered as , borrowed directly from the English word "turkey." The language that other languages pointed at for five hundred years eventually pointed right back at English. Which points at Turkey. Which points at India. Which points at Peru. Which uses Spanish, and calls it a peacock. The circle has no exit.

❧ X ❧

What the Bird Never Had

The turkey is, in many ways, the most conspicuously American of all animals. Benjamin Franklin, in a letter to his daughter, proposed it as a candidate for the national bird, arguing it was "a much more respectable Bird" than the bald eagle, "a Bird of Courage" that "would not hesitate to attack a Grenadier of the British Guards." It exists in the wild only in the Americas. It was on the land long before any European arrived.

Visual note, Franklin vs. the Eagle A warm, slightly playful illustrated portrait: Benjamin Franklin, in his characteristic spectacles and fur collar, flanked by a turkey on his left and a bald eagle on his right. The eagle looks imperious; the turkey looks skeptical. No caption needed.

And yet in five centuries of naming, no language that encountered the bird as an import gave it an American name. Every word points somewhere else.

The indigenous peoples of North America had their own vocabulary, of course, and it was varied and precise. The Wampanoag called the bird neyhom. The Narragansett said nahenan. The Algonkian of Long Island used nahiam. The Delaware had tshikenum. The Iroquois knew it as netachrochwa gatschinale. These names did not cross into any European language, despite English borrowing freely from indigenous vocabulary for raccoons, moose, opossums, and coyotes.

The indigenous words above are drawn from historical linguistic records. Several of these languages are now critically endangered or dormant. If you speak a language with a traditional word for the turkey that is not represented here, we encourage you to contribute a recording to Forvo.com, where native-speaker recordings are preserved and made freely available.

What got named was not the bird but the moment of encountering it: the surprise, the trade route, the misconception. Five hundred years of names, and not one of them quite got there.

A Final Note on Irony

In June 2022, the Republic of Turkey formally requested that the United Nations, and all international bodies, refer to it henceforth as Türkiye (pronounced approximately tur-key-YAY), the name it has used domestically in Turkish since the founding of the Republic in 1923. The UN accepted the change immediately. Most governments followed relatively quickly: Canada updated its official communications, as did most NATO allies. The ISO country code databases, diplomatic correspondence, and product labeling standards ("Made in Türkiye") were updated.

Major English-language media did not. The Associated Press style guide, a binding authority for most American newsrooms, was still "watching to see how much acceptance" the Turkish spelling gained as of 2023, and had not made a ruling. The Washington Post and CNN both explicitly stated they would continue using "Turkey" because it "best serves their audience." Reuters also held. Wikipedia kept its article under "Turkey." The BBC uses "Türkiye" in some contexts and "Turkey" in others. The name change, in short, was accepted at the official level and largely ignored at the popular one.

This means that, as of this writing, the country that changed its name to escape association with a bird is still called, in most English-language contexts, by the name of the bird. The bird is still called by the name of the country. And the country's own word for the bird still means "from India." The situation has not improved.

There is one further detail worth noting. Peru, the country whose name is used for the turkey in Portuguese, Hindi, Croatian, Slovenian, Hawaiian, and Rotuman, does not itself have a special word for the turkey. Peruvians use the Spanish , the word that means peacock. A country that gave its name to a bird across six languages calls that bird by the name of a completely different bird. The circle closes, and it closes wrong.

The bird was domesticated by the Aztecs, named after the Ottoman Empire, renamed after India, renamed after Peru, renamed after a city in India, renamed after France, renamed after Rome, renamed after Ethiopia, confused with a peacock, confused with a guinea fowl, and eventually returned, on the ships of English colonists, to the forests of North America, where it was once again seen by Europeans who called it by its wrong name. It has been living under assumed identities for five hundred years. It does not appear to mind.

A note on phonetic notation The symbols in grey on some of the word chips above are IPA, the International Phonetic Alphabet. Created in 1888, the IPA is a universal system that assigns one unique symbol to every distinct sound in every human language, completely independent of spelling. So /ˈtɜːrki/ always means exactly the same sound, whether you read it in Portugal, Japan, or Nigeria. Linguists, dictionaries, and language learners use it worldwide. On indigenous names: The Wampanoag, Narragansett, Algonkian, Delaware, and Iroquois words cited here come from historical linguistic records compiled in American Heritage, "Why It's Called a Turkey" (1984). Several of these languages are endangered or dormant. The Nahuatl word huehxōlōtl is still in active use in parts of Mexico.

On the scientific name: The etymology of Meleagris gallopavo is sourced from Cornell Lab of Ornithology's "The Key to Scientific Names" (Jobling); Monaco Nature Encyclopedia; and Wikipedia's "Wild turkey" article. The formal description is by Carl Linnaeus, Systema Naturae, 10th edition, 1758.

On why pavo did not spread: Spanish was indeed the first European language to encounter and name the bird, and Spanish pavo was in use from around 1519. The word did not spread because the bird was distributed across Europe largely through Portuguese and Ottoman trade intermediaries, who renamed it as they passed it along. By the time the turkey reached Northern Europe, the chain of custody had broken from Spanish. The Portuguese peru spread precisely because Portugal was running its own vast trade network independently and named the bird for its own geographical assumptions. See: WordReference Forums, "A turkey by any other name" (2010); The Week, James Harbeck (2015); United Noshes, "Talking Turkey" (2014).
